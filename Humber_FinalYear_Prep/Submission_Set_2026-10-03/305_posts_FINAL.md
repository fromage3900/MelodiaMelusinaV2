## Week 1 — "Ani History What and Why?"

### MAIN POST
History is decided by whoever holds the pen. In animation, that meant for decades it was mostly American studios and a handful of European names. The Japanese, Korean, and Indian animation traditions that ran parallel to Disney and Fleischer rarely showed up in the textbooks I grew up with. That is not because they did not exist. It is because the people writing the history were not looking at them.

Do we need this class to be good animators? Not strictly. You can learn the twelve principles from a YouTube video and practice them in Blender without ever knowing who Winsor McCay was. But I think you end up making the same mistakes the medium already solved. Every time I see a student reinvent a walk cycle from scratch, I wonder if they have ever studied the ones that came before. Not to copy, but to understand why they work.

The clearest example for me this term was studying Muybridge and early motion studies before I rigged a quadruped. I spent a week trying to work out how a sheep's legs should read in a walk cycle, then found that the whole problem — contact, down, pass, up — was already solved a hundred years ago. I had been inventing a wheel that had a name.

The class is not about memorizing dates. It is about knowing the conversation you are walking into when you animate.

## Week 2 — Historical Stereotypes

### MAIN POST
The class consensus seems to be that materials with historical stereotypes should still be shown, with context. I agree, but I think the context part is doing a lot of heavy lifting. It is easy to slap a disclaimer on a 1930s cartoon and call it handled. It is harder to actually sit with why that imagery was funny to audiences at the time, and what that says about the culture that produced it.

The Bobby Bumps cartoon came up in the thread. Watching it, the stereotypes are not subtle. They are the joke. But that is exactly why it is worth studying. It shows you what the audience accepted, what the studio thought they wanted, and how far the medium has come. Or has it. Some of the same visual shorthand shows up in modern work, just dressed up differently.

The modern version I keep coming back to is how games shorthand "foreign" or "evil" through the same tools — a dark palette, a broken accent, a costume that reads as "other" before the character says a word. It is the same logic as the old caricatures, just with a bigger budget and a texture artist. The clearest example I can think of is *Dark Souls*. Its enemies are almost never introduced as individuals. You get a silhouette, a sound cue, and a read on whether the thing is worth your stamina, and the design does the categorising before you have taken a swing. Compare that to a Disney villain, who is given a face, a name and a motive in the first two minutes precisely so that you can hate them specifically. Both approaches put an opinion about the character into the design before dialogue does. *Dark Souls* just declines to make that opinion explicit, which is arguably what makes it a much more interesting thing to study than the older version.

I do not think the answer is to bury these films. I think the answer is to watch them with open eyes and ask what they were really saying.

### REPLY TO A CLASSMATE
Your point about not being able to divorce art from its political and social implications is well taken. I think the danger in dismissing something as "of the time period" is that it lets the audience off the hook. If we just say "that was normal then," we do not have to ask whether it is still normal now, just in a different costume.

## Week 3 — "CENSORSHIP"

### MAIN POST
The professor asked us to avoid extreme statements, and I think that is the right call, because the extremes on both sides collapse under scrutiny. "No censorship at all" falls apart the moment you consider child exploitation material or direct incitement to violence. "Censor everything offensive" falls apart the moment you realize that every regime in history has used that language to silence dissent.

Where I land: censorship should protect, not manipulate. There is a difference between keeping harmful content away from minors and keeping inconvenient ideas away from everyone. The Hays Code is a good example. It was introduced to keep government censors out of Hollywood, which sounds good. But it also enforced a narrow moral code for decades, and the people who suffered under it were mostly minorities and anyone whose life did not fit the approved template.

The example I keep thinking about is the original *Star Trek* episode "Plato's Stepchildren," which fought to air the first interracial kiss on US television in 1968. The network tried to cut it, and the actors deliberately flubbed alternate takes so the censors had nothing usable. The thing the censor wanted to hide was two people in love. That is the whole argument in one story: the line between protect and manipulate is not drawn by the content, it is drawn by whoever holds the pen.

The hard part is that the line between protect and manipulate is drawn by whoever holds power. And power does not always draw it fairly.

### REPLY TO A CLASSMATE
I liked your point about the middle ground. I think the reason this topic is so hard is that both sides genuinely believe they are protecting people. The rating systems (ESRB, MPA) are a good example. They were created to protect kids, but they also create a framework where certain stories cannot be told to certain audiences at all. That is a form of censorship, even if a well-intentioned one.

## Week 4 — "Plan B...or C...or"

### MAIN POST
The professor shared that he wanted to perform in his early twenties and now loves being a teacher and writer. Many would say he gave up. I think that is the wrong frame. He did not stop performing. He found a different stage.

The Disney story is inspiring, but the professor is right that we do not hear about the thousands who persisted and never made it. Persistence is not a guarantee. It is a prerequisite. You need it, but it is not enough. The people who made it were persistent AND lucky AND in the right place at the right time. We celebrate the ones who made it and forget the ones who did everything right and still did not.

Do I have a plan B? Yes. I am a 3D animation student who can model, rig, light, and render. If the animation industry does not work out, those skills transfer to arch-viz, product visualization, and game asset creation. I am not excited about those paths the way I am about animation, but they are there. I think having a plan B is not giving up. It is making sure that if the dream changes, you do not fall with it.

My own plan B is the technical side of what I already do. I build tools as much as I build art — procedural placement, rigs, small Python utilities that regenerate assets on command. If character animation does not land, that tooling skill is a job on its own, and it is the part of the work I actually enjoy rather than just tolerate.

The win I am hoping for is not fame or money. It is making something that makes someone feel the way I felt watching *Spirited Away* for the first time. What gets me about that film is that nobody explains it to you. There is no character who turns to the camera and tells you this is a spirit world and here is how it works. The bathhouse just operates, the food turns into soot because the river gods are being forgotten, and the girl absorbs all of it and keeps going. The whole story runs on atmosphere and gesture instead of exposition, and it trusts the audience completely enough to let them figure it out. That is the part I want to be able to do. Not the scale of it, the trust.

### REPLY TO A CLASSMATE
Your point about adversity versus maturing is a good distinction. I think the difference between changing your dream and giving up is whether you are running toward something or away from something. If you are running away from failure, that is giving up. If you are running toward a different version of the same goal, that is maturing. The electrician plan B you mentioned is not giving up on animation. It is making sure you can pay rent while you keep working on your reel.
