## Week 1 - "Ani History What and Why?"

**Prompt:** What is History? Who gets to decide what events get included? Do we need an Animation History Class to be great, or even good, animators?

### Main Post (~200 words)

History is decided by whoever holds the pen. In animation, that meant for decades it was mostly American studios and a handful of European names. The Japanese, Korean, and Indian animation traditions that ran parallel to Disney and Fleischer rarely showed up in the textbooks I grew up with. That is not because they did not exist. It is because the people writing the history were not looking at them.

Do we need this class to be good animators? Not strictly. You can learn the twelve principles from a YouTube video and practice them in Blender without ever knowing who Winsor McCay was. But I think you end up making the same mistakes the medium already solved. Every time I see a student reinvent a walk cycle from scratch, I wonder if they have ever studied the ones that came before. Not to copy, but to understand why they work.

## Week 2 - Historical Stereotypes

**Prompt (inferred):** How should we handle historical stereotypes in animation? Should they be shown with context, or dismissed?

### Main Post (~200 words)

The class consensus seems to be that materials with historical stereotypes should still be shown, with context. I agree, but I think the context part is doing a lot of heavy lifting. It is easy to slap a disclaimer on a 1930s cartoon and call it handled. It is harder to actually sit with why that imagery was funny to audiences at the time, and what that says about the culture that produced it.

The Bobby Bumps cartoon came up in the thread. Watching it, the stereotypes are not subtle. They are the joke. But that is exactly why it is worth studying. It shows you what the audience accepted, what the studio thought they wanted, and how far the medium has come. Or has it. Some of the same visual shorthand shows up in modern work, just dressed up differently.

## Week 3 - "CENSORSHIP"

**Prompt:** Censorship has always been a sensitive topic. Avoid extreme, all-inclusive statements. Think about your own thoughts on this challenging topic.

### Main Post (~220 words)

The professor asked us to avoid extreme statements, and I think that is the right call, because the extremes on both sides collapse under scrutiny. "No censorship at all" falls apart the moment you consider child exploitation material or direct incitement to violence. "Censor everything offensive" falls apart the moment you realize that every regime in history has used that language to silence dissent.

Where I land: censorship should protect, not manipulate. There is a difference between keeping harmful content away from minors and keeping inconvenient ideas away from everyone. The Hays Code is a good example. It was introduced to keep government censors out of Hollywood, which sounds good. But it also enforced a narrow moral code for decades, and the people who suffered under it were mostly minorities and anyone whose life did not fit the approved template.

[your example: a specific film, game, or show that was censored or banned, and whether you think the censorship was justified]

The hard part is that the line between protect and manipulate is drawn by whoever holds power. And power does not always draw it fairly.

### Reply to Classmate (~80 words)

I liked your point about the middle ground. I think the reason this topic is so hard is that both sides genuinely believe they are protecting people. The rating systems (ESRB, MPA) are a good example. They were created to protect kids, but they also create a framework where certain stories cannot be told to certain audiences at all. That is a form of censorship, even if a well-intentioned one.
